# Canada Citizenship

## Overview

:::callout
**3 years (~1095 days)** of physical presence in Canada within the **5 years** before you apply makes you eligible
for citizenship. Time before permanent residence counts at **half a day each**, capped at **365 credited days**. You
also need tax filings for **3 of those 5 years**, language and a knowledge test.
:::

| Key parameters          |                                            |
|:------------------------|-------------------------------------------:|
| Presence                |                       3 years (~1095 days) |
| Qualifying period       |                                    5 years |
| Counting                | Any part of a day, half-day credit temp PR |
| Additional requirements |       Tax filing, language, knowledge test |

## Understanding the rule

This applies to [permanent residents](/rules/canada-permanent-residency) applying for citizenship, whatever their nationality or how they became a
permanent resident.

What counts is days you were **physically in Canada**. Ordinary time abroad — holidays, work trips, family visits —
doesn't count, however strong your ties to Canada stay.

One narrow exception exists. Days abroad count if you were a permanent resident employed full-time by the Canadian
Armed Forces, the federal public administration, or a provincial or territorial public service. The same credit covers
time abroad with a spouse, common-law partner or parent — a Canadian citizen or permanent resident — serving in one of
those roles. People engaged locally at an overseas post don't qualify.

The half-day credit is what makes the arithmetic interesting. Time in Canada before you became a permanent resident —
as a student, worker, or protected person — counts at **half rate**, capped at **365 credited days**. Someone who
studied in Canada for two years can therefore reach permanent residence already holding a year of credit toward
citizenship.

Presence isn't the only requirement. You must have **filed income taxes** for at least 3 of the 5 years, in any year
you were required to file. Applicants aged 18 to 54 must also prove basic speaking and listening ability in English or
French, and pass a **knowledge test** on Canada.

## How to keep track

1. You need **3 years (~1095 days)** physically in Canada within the **5 years** immediately before the date you apply.
2. **Any part of a day** in Canada counts as a full day, including arrival and departure days.
3. Days in Canada **before** you became a permanent resident count at **half a day each**, and
   **no more than 365 credited days** can come from this source however long you were here.
4. The window counts **backwards from your application date**, so waiting longer moves the window and can bring more
   qualifying days into scope.
5. Time abroad counts only for a permanent resident serving overseas with the Canadian Armed Forces, the federal public
   administration or a provincial or territorial public service — or for family accompanying them. Every other day
   outside Canada counts for nothing.

Keep a full record of every entry and exit across the five years, plus evidence of any pre-permanent-residence status
you're claiming half-days for. Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada (IRCC) publishes a physical presence
calculator, and running your dates through it before applying is far safer than counting by hand.

## Edge cases

- **The half-day credit is capped regardless of how long you were here.** Four years of pre-permanent-residence study
  yields the same 365 credited days as two years does.
- **The tax condition is checked against tax records, not your word.** Applicants authorise the Canada Revenue Agency
  (CRA) to share their filing history directly with IRCC, so an unfiled year surfaces on its own.
- **Time in immigration detention or serving a sentence generally doesn't count**, and can also bar an application
  outright for a period.
- **Applying with barely enough days is risky.** Processing takes months and presence is assessed as at the application
  date, but a miscounted trip leaves no margin — applying with a buffer of extra days is the practical protection.

## If you get this rule wrong

A refusal on presence grounds costs the fee and the wait, and you reapply once you've accumulated the days — the practical cost is time rather than a permanent bar. A refusal involving misrepresentation is a different matter: it carries a five-year bar on reapplying and can put your permanent residence itself at risk, since misrepresentation is separately grounds for losing status. **Professional advice is strongly recommended in situations like this.**

## Examples

### Three settled years after landing

You became a permanent resident four years ago and have lived in Canada continuously since, travelling abroad about
three weeks a year. Your physical presence is well past 1,095 days and your taxes are filed, so the presence
requirement is comfortably met.

### Student years that shorten the wait

You studied in Canada for two years before becoming a permanent resident. Those days count at half rate up to the
365-day cap, so you arrive at permanent residence with a year of credit and need roughly two more years of presence
rather than three.

### Years abroad with a Canadian employer

You spent three of the last five years abroad working for a Canadian bank. The employer being Canadian changes nothing
here — the credit is only for the armed forces and public service — so those days don't count and you're far short of
1,095.

## FAQ

### How long do I have to live in Canada before applying for citizenship?

1,095 days of physical presence within the 5 years before you apply. Time in Canada before becoming a permanent
resident counts at half a day each, capped at 365 credited days.

### Does time outside Canada ever count toward citizenship?

Rarely. Days abroad count only if you were a permanent resident serving overseas with the Canadian Armed Forces, the
federal public administration or a provincial or territorial public service. Accompanying a spouse, partner or parent
in one of those roles counts too. Ordinary work or family time abroad counts for nothing.

### How does the half-day credit work?

Each day you spent in Canada before becoming a permanent resident — as a student, worker or protected person — counts
as half a day, and no more than 365 credited days can come from that source in total.

### Do I need to have filed taxes?

Yes, for at least 3 of the 5 years in the qualifying period, where you were required to file. It's a separate condition
from the day count and is checked independently.

### Is this the same as the rule for keeping my PR status?

No, and they're easy to confuse. Permanent residency needs 730 days in 5 years and counts certain time abroad.
Citizenship needs 1,095 days and, apart from the narrow public-service case, counts only physical presence in Canada.

### What happens after I get citizenship?

Canadian citizenship isn't subject to a residence obligation, so the day counting ends. Canada also permits dual
citizenship, so you don't have to give up another nationality to hold it.
