# Canada Citizenship (1,095-day rule)

## Overview

:::callout
Applying for Canadian citizenship needs **1,095 days** — three years — of physical presence in Canada within the
**5 years** before you apply. Time spent in Canada *before* becoming a permanent resident can count too, at **half a
day each**, up to a maximum of 365 days credited. You also have to have filed Canadian taxes for at least 3 of those
5 years, where required to.
:::

| Key facts               |                                              |
|:------------------------|---------------------------------------------:|
| Absence limit           |             1,095 days required in Canada |
| Qualifying period       |                                      5 years |
| Period / Window         |           5 years back from application date |
| Alternative             |     Pre-PR days at half rate, 365-day cap |
| Additional requirements | Tax filing 3 of 5 years, language, knowledge |

## Understanding the rule

The citizenship test is markedly stricter than the one for keeping permanent residence, and the two are often confused.
Permanent residency asks for 730 days in five years and counts certain time abroad. Citizenship asks for 1,095 days and
counts only days you were **physically in Canada** — no accompanying-a-spouse provision, no credit for working abroad
for a Canadian employer.

The half-day credit is what makes the arithmetic interesting. Time in Canada before you became a permanent resident —
as a student, worker, or protected person — counts at **half rate**, capped at **365 credited days**. Someone who
studied in Canada for two years before landing can therefore arrive at permanent residence already holding a year of
credit toward citizenship.

Two conditions sit alongside the day count. You must have **filed income taxes** for at least 3 of the 5 years, where
you were required to file. And applicants in a defined age band must meet **language** and **knowledge of Canada**
requirements, both tested.

## How to keep track

1. You need **1,095 days** physically in Canada within the **5 years** immediately before the date you apply.
2. **Any part of a day** in Canada counts as a full day, including arrival and departure days.
3. Days in Canada **before** you became a permanent resident count at **half a day each**, and no more than **365
   credited days** can come from this source however long you were here.
4. The window counts **backwards from your application date**, so waiting longer moves the window and can bring more
   qualifying days into scope.
5. Only physical presence counts — unlike the permanent residency obligation, no time outside Canada counts toward this
   total for any reason.

Keep a full record of every entry and exit across the five years, plus evidence of any pre-permanent-residence status
you're claiming half-days for. Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada publishes a physical presence calculator,
and running your dates through it before applying is far safer than counting by hand.

## Edge cases

- **The citizenship and permanent residency tests are different rules.** Meeting the 730-day residency obligation
  says nothing about meeting 1,095 days for citizenship, and time abroad that preserved your permanent residence
  contributes nothing here.
- **The half-day credit is capped regardless of how long you were here.** Four years of pre-permanent-residence study
  yields the same 365 credited days as two years does.
- **Time in immigration detention or serving a sentence generally doesn't count**, and can also bar an application
  outright for a period.
- **Applying with barely enough days is risky.** Processing takes months and presence is assessed as at the application
  date, but a miscounted trip leaves no margin — applying with a buffer of extra days is the practical protection.

## If you get this rule wrong

A refusal on presence grounds costs the fee and the wait, and you reapply once you've accumulated the days — the practical cost is time rather than a permanent bar. A refusal involving misrepresentation is a different matter: it carries a five-year bar on reapplying and can put your permanent residence itself at risk, since misrepresentation is separately grounds for losing status. **Professional immigration advice is strongly recommended in situations like this.**

## Examples

### Three settled years after landing

You became a permanent resident four years ago and have lived in Canada continuously since, travelling abroad about
three weeks a year. Your physical presence is well past 1,095 days and your taxes are filed, so the presence
requirement is comfortably met.

### Student years that shorten the wait

You studied in Canada for two years before becoming a permanent resident. Those days count at half rate up to the
365-day cap, so you arrive at permanent residence with a year of credit and need roughly two more years of presence
rather than three.

### Meeting the PR obligation but not the citizenship one

You spent three of the last five years abroad accompanying your Canadian citizen spouse. That time preserved your
permanent residency, but none of it counts toward citizenship, where only physical presence in Canada is credited.

## FAQ

### How long do I have to live in Canada before applying for citizenship?

1,095 days of physical presence within the 5 years before you apply. Time in Canada before becoming a permanent
resident counts at half a day each, capped at 365 credited days.

### Does time outside Canada ever count toward citizenship?

No. Unlike the permanent residency obligation, only days physically in Canada count. Accompanying a Canadian spouse
abroad or working overseas for a Canadian employer contributes nothing here.

### How does the half-day credit work?

Each day you spent in Canada before becoming a permanent resident — as a student, worker or protected person — counts
as half a day, and no more than 365 credited days can come from that source in total.

### Do I need to have filed taxes?

Yes, for at least 3 of the 5 years in the qualifying period, where you were required to file. It's a separate condition
from the day count and is checked independently.

### Is this the same as the rule for keeping my PR status?

No, and they're easy to confuse. Permanent residency needs 730 days in 5 years and counts certain time abroad.
Citizenship needs 1,095 days and counts only physical presence in Canada.

### What happens after I get citizenship?

Canadian citizenship isn't subject to a residence obligation, so the day counting ends. Canada also permits dual
citizenship, so you don't have to give up another nationality to hold it.
