# Bulgaria Tax Residency (183-day rule)

## Overview

:::callout
Spending **more than 183 days** in Bulgaria across **any 12-month** period makes you a tax resident, counted from the
calendar year in which you passed day 183. A **centre of vital interests** there qualifies you instead, with **no day
count** at all.
:::

| Key parameters          |                                |
|:------------------------|-------------------------------:|
| Threshold               |                       183 days |
| Period / Window         |              Rolling 12 months |
| Counting                |              Any part of a day |
| Alternative             | Centre of vital interests test |
| Additional requirements |    Permanent address with ties |

## Understanding the rule

Bulgaria has 2 practical routes into tax residency, and the day count is only the first.

The **183-day rule** measures presence across a 12-month period rather than a fixed calendar year, while the residency
it produces attaches to the calendar year in which you passed the threshold. Those 2 things are easy to conflate: a
rolling measurement producing a calendar-year status.

The **centre of vital interests** is the second route, and it needs no days at all. It looks at where your personal
and economic life is anchored — family, property, the place you work from or run a business, the place you manage your
assets from. Someone whose life is plainly based in Bulgaria can be resident on a very low day count.

A **permanent address** in Bulgaria is often assumed to be a third route. It is not, on its own. An address only makes
you resident where your centre of vital interests is also in the country, so registering one changes nothing by
itself.

## How to keep track

1. The threshold is **more than 183 days** of presence within any **12-month period**, so 183 exactly is not enough.
2. **Any part of a day** counts as a full day. Entry and departure days are each treated separately as days of stay in
   the country.
3. The window **rolls** rather than resetting with a new year, so check the worst 12-month stretch your travel
   produces rather than each calendar year alone.
4. Note the calendar year in which you cross day 183, because that is the year the residency attaches to even though
   the count spans a rolling window.
5. Track your ties as well as your days — a home, family, or a business run from Bulgaria can settle residency without
   the day count going anywhere near the threshold.

Keep entry and exit records across at least 2 calendar years, plus evidence of where your personal and economic life
sits if you may fall on the vital-interests limb.

## Edge cases

- **The rolling window and the calendar year do different jobs.** The count runs across any 12 months, but the
  resulting status lands in the calendar year the threshold was crossed in.
- **A permanent address alone doesn't make you resident.** Registering an address in Bulgaria only matters where your
  centre of vital interests is there too, so it isn't a shortcut on its own.
- **You can be resident on a low day count.** Where family, property and business all sit in Bulgaria, the
  vital-interests limb can attach even to someone who spends most of the year elsewhere.
- **A tax residency certificate is issued on evidence, not on request.** The National Revenue Agency treats the day
  count as the strongest basis for issuing one, so the vital-interests route can be harder to document.
- **Bulgaria taxes residents at a flat rate on worldwide income.** The status changes the scope of the charge rather
  than the rate, which is the same for residents and nonresidents on Bulgarian-source income.

## If you get this rule wrong

Residents are taxed on worldwide income while nonresidents pay only on Bulgarian-source income, so an incorrect position usually surfaces as foreign earnings left off a return. Late filing attracts a fine, and unpaid tax carries interest calculated daily at the European Central Bank main refinancing rate plus 8 percentage points, running from the original due date until the balance is cleared. **Professional tax advice is strongly recommended in situations like this.**

## Examples

### A rolling window that crosses a year end

You arrive in Sofia in August and stay through to the following July. No calendar year holds more than 183 days on its
own, but the rolling 12-month window does, and you become resident for the calendar year in which you crossed day 183.

### Ties that decide it without the days

You spend about 120 days a year in Bulgaria, but your spouse and children live there, you own the family home, and you
run your business from it. The day count falls short, yet the centre of vital interests makes you resident.

### An address that changes nothing

You register a permanent address in Bulgaria for administrative convenience but live and work in another country, with
no family or business there. The address alone doesn't make you resident, and neither test is met.

## FAQ

### Is the Bulgarian 183-day count based on the calendar year?

No. It is measured across any 12-month period, so the window rolls. The residency that results is then assigned to the
calendar year in which you passed the 183rd day.

### How are days in Bulgaria counted?

Any part of a day counts as a full day, and the days of entering and leaving the country are each regarded separately
as days of stay.

### Does a permanent address in Bulgaria make me tax resident?

Not on its own. An address only counts where your centre of vital interests is also in Bulgaria, so registering one
without moving your life there changes nothing.

### Can I be resident without reaching 183 days?

Yes. If your centre of vital interests is in Bulgaria — judged on family, property, and where you work from or manage
your assets — you can be resident on a much lower day count.

### What does Bulgaria tax a resident on?

Worldwide income, at a flat rate. Nonresidents are taxed only on income arising in Bulgaria, at the same rate.

### How do I get a Bulgarian tax residency certificate?

You apply to the National Revenue Agency, which issues it on evidence rather than on request. The day count is
treated as the strongest basis, so the vital-interests route can be harder to document.
