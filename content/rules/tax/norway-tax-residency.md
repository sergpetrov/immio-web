# Norway Tax Residency (183-day and 270-day rules)

## Overview

:::callout
You're a Norwegian tax resident once you spend **more than 183 days** in Norway in any twelve-month period, or **more
than 270 days** across any thirty-six months — either one is enough. Both windows roll continuously instead of
resetting each January, so a habit of long visits can trip the second test even when no single year comes close.
Ending residency is a separate hurdle with its own conditions, and it doesn't happen just because you move away.
:::

| Key facts               |                                      |
|:------------------------|-------------------------------------:|
| Thresholds              |                   184 days, 271 days |
| Period / Window         | Rolling 12 months, rolling 36 months |
| Counting                |                    Any part of a day |
| Additional requirements |     Emigration test to end residency |

## Understanding the rule

You are a Norwegian tax resident if you meet either of two presence tests, in any order:

- **183-day rule** — more than 183 days in Norway within any twelve-month period.
- **270-day rule** — more than 270 days in Norway within any thirty-six-month period.

Meeting either makes you a Norwegian tax resident, taxed on worldwide income and worldwide wealth. Falling short of
both makes you a nonresident, generally taxed only on Norwegian-source income.

Neither window is tied to the calendar. They roll continuously, so the twelve or thirty-six months are measured from
whatever point makes the total highest, not from 1 January. When residency begins then depends on which test you trip
and how the days fall:

- Pass 183 days inside a single calendar year, and you're resident from your **first day** in Norway that year.
- Pass 183 days on a stay that straddles a year-end, and you're resident from **1 January of the second year**.
- Pass 270 days over thirty-six months, and you're resident from **1 January of the year** in which you crossed it.

Ending residency is a separate test, and leaving is not enough on its own. You have to take up permanent residence
abroad and keep your stays in Norway to no more than 61 days in the income year. On top of that, no dwelling in Norway
may be available to you or your immediate family. If you were resident for ten years or more, those conditions have to
hold for three consecutive income years, and residency then ends from 1 January of the fourth.

## How to keep track

1. The thresholds are **more than 183 days** in any twelve-month period and **more than 270 days** in any
   thirty-six-month period. Either one is enough on its own.
2. **Any part of a day** in Norway counts as a whole day, arrival and departure days included, and the reason for the
   visit makes no difference.
3. The days don't have to be consecutive, and neither window resets on 1 January — you have to check rolling periods
   rather than each calendar year in isolation.
4. If you're trying to end residency instead, the number that matters is **61 days** in the income year, counted the
   same way.
5. Keep travel records going several years back, since the thirty-six-month window reaches much further than most
   people track. Add tenancy or sale documents for any Norwegian home, plus proof of permanent residence abroad, if
   you're relying on the emigration test.

## Edge cases

- **Moving away doesn't end your tax residency by itself.** Residency runs until the emigration conditions are
  actually met, and people who were resident for ten years or more face a three-year wait on top of that.
- **A home available to your family counts as available to you.** For the emigration test, a dwelling in Norway open to
  your spouse, partner or children blocks the exit even if you never set foot in it.
- **The 270-day rule catches regular visitors who never come close in one year.** Just over 90 days a year for three
  years is enough, even though no single year gets near 183.
- **Residency brings wealth tax with it, not just income tax.** Residents are taxed on worldwide wealth as well as
  worldwide income, which is a bigger change than most new arrivals expect.
- **A simplified flat-rate scheme for foreign workers is not a residency test.** It's a way of taxing employment
  income, and the day counts keep running underneath it regardless.
- **Dual residence is possible.** Another country can treat you as resident under its own rules, and a treaty
  tiebreaker then assigns a single treaty residence without cancelling your Norwegian domestic residency.

## If you get this rule wrong

An incorrect residency position that understates your tax normally attracts additional tax at 20% of the amount
involved. That drops to 10% where a third party such as an employer had already reported the missing information, and
rises by a further 20% or 40% where the error was intentional or grossly negligent. A voluntary correction — frivillig
retting — made before the Tax Administration starts looking removes the additional tax and avoids a police report,
though the tax and interest are still payable. **Professional tax advice is strongly recommended in situations like this.**

## Examples

### A contract that clears the line in one year

You take a nine-month posting in Stavanger starting in February and are in Norway for 250 days by the end of December.
You pass 183 days inside a single calendar year, so you're a Norwegian tax resident from your first day in the country
that year.

### Regular visits that stay under both windows

You spend 80 days in Norway each summer for three years — 240 days in total. No rolling twelve-month window reaches
184 days, and the thirty-six-month total stays under 271, so both tests fail and you remain a nonresident.

### A clean departure blocked by an empty house

After twelve years in Norway you move abroad permanently and cut your visits to 30 days a year, but leave the family
house standing empty and available. You are comfortably inside the 61-day limit, yet a Norwegian home still available
to you means your residency doesn't end.

## FAQ

### Is Norwegian tax residency just a 183-day rule?

No. There is a second, longer-range test: more than 270 days across any thirty-six-month period. Someone who visits
for a few months every year can become resident under that one without ever passing 183 days in a single year.

### How are days in Norway counted?

Any part of a day spent in Norway counts as a whole day, including the days you arrive and leave. The days don't need
to run consecutively, and it makes no difference why you were there — work, study, and holidays all count the same.

### When does my Norwegian tax residency actually start?

It depends on how the days fall. Passing 183 days within one calendar year makes you resident from your first day in
Norway that year. If the stay straddles a year-end, or you trip the 270-day rule, residency starts on 1 January of the
relevant year instead.

### Does moving out of Norway end my tax residency?

Not on its own. You have to settle permanently abroad, stay no more than 61 days in Norway in the income year, and
have no dwelling there available to you or your immediate family. After ten years or more of residency, those
conditions must hold for three consecutive years first.

### What happens if I meet either test?

You become a Norwegian tax resident, taxed on **worldwide income and worldwide wealth** rather than only
Norwegian-source income. Failing both tests instead leaves you a nonresident, generally taxed only on income arising
in Norway.

### Can I be a Norwegian tax resident and a tax resident of another country at the same time?

Yes. The Norwegian tests only settle your position under Norwegian law, and other countries apply their own. A double
tax treaty between Norway and that country would then decide which one takes priority for specific income.
