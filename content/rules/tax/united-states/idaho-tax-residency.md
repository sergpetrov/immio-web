# Idaho Tax Residency (270-day rule)

## Overview

:::callout
Spending **more than 270 days** in Idaho while keeping a **place of abode** there for the **entire tax year** makes
you a resident, taxed on income from all sources. Being **domiciled** in Idaho for the whole year qualifies you with
no day count. **Any part of a day** counts unless that day was temporary or transitory.
:::

| Key parameters          |                            |
|:------------------------|---------------------------:|
| Threshold               |                   270 days |
| Period / Window         |  Tax year (1 Jan – 31 Dec) |
| Counting                |          Any part of a day |
| Alternative             |                   Domicile |
| Additional requirements | Abode kept the entire year |

## Understanding the rule

This applies to anyone keeping a home in Idaho year-round, whichever state they treat as their own. You are an Idaho
resident if either of these applies:

- **Domicile for the entire tax year** — Idaho is your permanent home for all twelve months, not merely part of them.
- **Abode plus days** — you maintain a place of abode in Idaho for the entire tax year and spend more than 270 days
  of that year in the state.

Idaho sets the highest day threshold of any state that uses one. More than 270 days is roughly nine months, which
leaves far more room than the 183-day line used by most income-tax states.

The abode condition is correspondingly strict. It must be maintained for the **entire** tax year rather than for
substantially all of it or for six months, so acquiring or giving up an Idaho home partway through the year defeats
the test regardless of how the day count falls.

Both limbs also carry an all-or-nothing quality on timing: domicile counts only if it held for the whole year, and
the abode only if it was in place throughout.

## How to keep track

1. The threshold is **more than 270 days** in the tax year, which runs 1 January to 31 December, so day **271** is
   the first that qualifies.
2. **Presence for any part of a calendar day counts as a day spent in the state**, unless you can show your presence
   that day was for a temporary or transitory purpose.
3. That exception is per-day rather than per-stay, so a day passing through can be excluded while the rest of a trip
   still counts.
4. Days are counted **in the aggregate** across the year, not as one continuous stay.
5. Check the abode against the **entire tax year** requirement first. A home bought in February or sold in November
   fails it, whatever your count.

Keep day-level travel records for the whole year, along with documents showing the exact dates any Idaho home was
available to you and evidence for any day you treat as transitory.

## Edge cases

- **The abode must cover all twelve months.** Unlike states asking for substantially all of the year, Idaho requires
  the entire tax year, so a mid-year purchase or sale defeats the limb.
- **Individual days can be excluded.** Presence for a temporary or transitory purpose on a given day does not count,
  which is a per-day exception rather than a whole-stay one.
- **270 days is a high bar.** Someone can spend two-thirds of the year in Idaho and still fall outside the statutory
  route entirely.
- **Domicile must also cover the full year.** Being domiciled in Idaho for only part of the year puts you outside
  that route, though a part-year filing may still be required.
- **Falling outside both routes is not the end of it.** Idaho-source income remains taxable whatever your residency
  status.

## If you get this rule wrong

A resident is taxed on income from all sources while a nonresident pays only on Idaho-source income, so an error usually surfaces as out-of-state earnings never reported while another state taxed the same income. The Tax Commission charges interest from the original due date and adds penalties for filing late and for underpaying, with higher exposure where an understatement is treated as deliberate. **Professional tax advice is strongly recommended in situations like this.**

## Examples

### A year-round home and a long stay

You are domiciled in Nevada, own a house in Boise available to you all year, and spend about 290 days in Idaho. Both
limbs hold, so you are an Idaho resident for the year.

### A high count without a full-year home

You spend 300 days in Idaho but only buy your house there in March. The abode was not maintained for the entire tax
year, so the statutory route fails despite an ample day count.

### A full-year home and a shorter stay

You keep an Idaho cabin available all year and spend around 250 days in the state. The abode limb holds but the count
falls short of 270, so the rule does not attach.

## FAQ

### Why is Idaho's threshold 270 days rather than 183?

Its statute simply sets the line higher. More than 270 days is roughly nine months, the most generous day threshold
of any state that uses one.

### Does my Idaho home need to be available all year?

Yes. The place of abode must be maintained for the entire tax year, which is stricter than the "substantially all"
standard used in states like New York.

### Do partial days count in Idaho?

Yes. Presence for any part of a calendar day counts as a day in the state, unless you can show that day's presence
was for a temporary or transitory purpose.

### Can I exclude individual days?

Yes, where presence on that particular day was temporary or transitory. The exception applies day by day rather than
to a whole trip.

### What if I bought my Idaho home mid-year?

The abode limb is not met for that tax year, because the statute requires the home to be maintained for the entire
year. A high day count does not compensate.

### Am I taxed by Idaho if I fall outside both routes?

Only on Idaho-source income. Residents are taxed on income from all sources wherever earned.
