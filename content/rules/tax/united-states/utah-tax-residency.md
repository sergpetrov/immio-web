# Utah Tax Residency (183-day rule)

## Overview

:::callout
Maintaining a **place of abode** in Utah and spending **183 days or more** there in a **tax year** makes you
domiciled, and so a resident taxed on income from all sources. A **day** counts only where you spent **more time in
Utah** than in any other state.
:::

| Key parameters          |                           |
|:------------------------|--------------------------:|
| Threshold               |                  183 days |
| Period / Window         | Tax year (1 Jan – 31 Dec) |
| Counting                | Days mostly spent in Utah |
| Alternative             |                  Domicile |
| Additional requirements |    Place of abode in Utah |

## Understanding the rule

This applies to anyone with somewhere to live in Utah, whichever state they treat as their own. Utah decides
residence through domicile, and reaches it two ways:

- **Abode plus days** — you maintain a place of abode in Utah and spend 183 days or more of the tax year there.
  Meeting both is treated as domicile in the state.
- **The domicile tests themselves** — a tiered set of presumptions and, where those do not settle it, a weighing of
  the wider circumstances.

Utah counts days unlike almost anywhere else. A day counts toward the 183 only where you spent **more time in Utah
than in any other state**, so a day split across state lines falls to whichever state got the larger share. That is
considerably stricter than the any-part-of-a-day rule most states apply, and it means many travel days do not count
at all.

Domicile works through layered presumptions rather than open-ended judgment. Claiming the Utah **residential
property tax exemption** on a home raises a rebuttable presumption of domicile. Other markers, such as a child
enrolled in a Utah public school or being registered to vote in the state, feed the same analysis. Where the
presumptions do not settle it, the Tax Commission weighs the full circumstances on the balance of the evidence.

The threshold is set at **183 or more** rather than more than 183, so day 183 itself qualifies.

## How to keep track

1. The threshold is **183 days or more** in the tax year, so day **183** itself qualifies.
2. A day counts only where you spent **more time in Utah than in any other state**. Track where the hours fell, not
   merely whether you set foot in Utah — a short stop on the way elsewhere does not count.
3. Both limbs must hold in the same tax year: a **place of abode** in Utah and the days.
4. Watch the **property tax exemption** separately. Claiming the residential exemption on a Utah home presumes
   domicile regardless of your day count.
5. Note the other domicile markers — a child in a Utah public school, voter registration — since they feed the
   analysis even where no presumption attaches on its own.

Keep day-level travel records for the whole year, plus records of any Utah property tax exemption claimed, school
enrolment and voter registration.

## Edge cases

- **183 qualifies, not 184.** Utah sets the line at 183 or more days, so a count that would fall short elsewhere is
  enough here.
- **A split day goes to whichever state got more of it.** Because a day counts only where Utah took the larger share
  of your time, days spent crossing state lines frequently fall outside the count altogether.
- **A property tax exemption presumes domicile.** Claiming the residential exemption on a Utah home raises a
  rebuttable presumption that you are domiciled there, whatever your days.
- **Domicile is tiered, not a single question.** Presumptions apply first, and only where they do not settle it does
  the Commission weigh the wider circumstances.
- **The abode is required, not optional.** A high day count with nothing maintained in Utah does not meet this route,
  however long the stay.

## If you get this rule wrong

A resident is taxed on income from all sources while a nonresident pays only on Utah-source income, so an error usually surfaces as out-of-state earnings never reported by someone who miscounted days that in fact fell to another state. The Tax Commission charges interest from the original due date and adds penalties for filing late and for underpaying, with greater exposure where an understatement is treated as deliberate. **Professional tax advice is strongly recommended in situations like this.**

## Examples

### A rented condo and a long season

You are domiciled in California, rent a Park City condo month to month, and spend about 200 days there with most of
each day in Utah. The abode and the days both hold, so Utah treats you as domiciled.

### A count landing exactly on the line

You keep a Utah apartment and record exactly 183 qualifying days. Because the rule is 183 or more, that is enough,
where the same count would fall short in a state written as "more than 183."

### A property exemption that presumes domicile

You spend only 60 days in Utah but claim the residential property tax exemption on a house there. That raises a
rebuttable presumption of Utah domicile despite the low count.

## FAQ

### Is the Utah threshold 183 or 184 days?

183. The rule is 183 or more days, so day 183 itself qualifies, a day earlier than states written as "more than 183
days."

### Do I need a home in Utah for the day rule to apply?

Yes. The route needs both a place of abode in Utah and 183 days or more in the same tax year. Neither on its own is
enough.

### How does Utah count a day?

Only a day where you spent more time in Utah than in any other state counts toward the 183. This is stricter than the
any-part-of-a-day rule most states use, so a brief stop while traveling elsewhere does not add to the count.

### Does claiming a Utah property tax exemption affect my residency?

Yes. Claiming the residential property tax exemption on a Utah home raises a rebuttable presumption that you are
domiciled in the state, independently of your day count.

### How does Utah decide domicile?

Through a tiered set of presumptions rather than a single question of intent. Where the presumptions do not settle
it, the Tax Commission weighs the wider circumstances on the balance of the evidence.

### Do school enrolment and voter registration matter?

Yes. A child enrolled in a Utah public school and registration to vote in the state both feed the domicile analysis.
