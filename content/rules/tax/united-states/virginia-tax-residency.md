# Virginia Tax Residency (183-day actual residency rule)

## Overview

:::callout
Virginia has **2 classes of resident**. You are an **actual resident** if you maintained a **place of abode** in the
state for an aggregate of **more than 183 days** of the tax year, and a **domiciliary resident** if Virginia is your
permanent home. Part of a day counts, except when spent **solely in transit**.
:::

| Key parameters          |                                     |
|:------------------------|------------------------------------:|
| Threshold               |                            183 days |
| Period / Window         |           Tax year (1 Jan – 31 Dec) |
| Counting                | Any part of a day, transit excluded |
| Alternative             |                            Domicile |
| Additional requirements |          Place of abode in Virginia |

## Understanding the rule

This applies to anyone with a place to stay in Virginia, whichever state they treat as their own. Virginia is unusual
in naming its two routes as separate classes of resident:

- **Domiciliary resident** — Virginia is your permanent place of residence, the place you intend to return to even
  while living elsewhere.
- **Actual resident** — you maintained a place of abode in Virginia for an aggregate of more than 183 days of the tax
  year, whatever your domicile.

Both classes are taxed as residents. Someone can be an actual resident of Virginia and a domiciliary resident of
another state in the same year, and both states will treat the year as a resident year.

Virginia also excludes something most states do not. A day counts if you were physically present for any portion of
it, **except** where that part-day was spent solely while in transit to a destination outside Virginia. Passing
through on the way somewhere else does not add to the count.

Changing domicile requires two things together: abandoning the Virginia domicile with no intention of returning, and
acquiring a new one by being physically present somewhere else intending to stay permanently or indefinitely.

## How to keep track

1. The threshold is an aggregate of **more than 183 days** in the tax year, so day **184** is the first that
   qualifies. The year runs 1 January to 31 December.
2. **Any portion of a day counts as a day**, with one exception: a part-day spent solely in transit to a destination
   outside Virginia is excluded.
3. That exclusion is narrow. It covers passing through, not a stopover that turns into time spent in the state.
4. Days are counted **in the aggregate** across the year rather than as one continuous stay.
5. Track the **place of abode** alongside the days, since the actual residency class turns on maintaining one in
   Virginia rather than on presence alone.

Keep day-level travel records for the whole year, along with itineraries able to show which days were pure transit
and documents covering any Virginia accommodation.

## Edge cases

- **Transit days are excluded, but only pure transit.** A part-day spent solely on the way to somewhere outside
  Virginia does not count, while a day that includes any other activity does.
- **Two classes, one tax treatment.** Actual residents and domiciliary residents are taxed the same way, so the
  distinction matters for how you are caught rather than for what you pay.
- **You can be an actual resident here and domiciled elsewhere.** Two states can treat the same year as a
  resident year, with credits closing only part of the gap.
- **Leaving needs a new domicile, not just an absence.** Abandoning Virginia is not enough on its own; you must
  acquire a domicile somewhere else.
- **Northern Virginia commuting is the common trap.** Regular short crossings from the District or Maryland add full
  days unless they were purely transit.

## If you get this rule wrong

A resident is taxed on income from all sources while a nonresident pays only on Virginia-source income, so an error usually surfaces as out-of-state earnings never reported while another state taxed the same income. The Department charges interest from the original due date and adds penalties for filing late and for underpaying, with greater exposure where an understatement is treated as deliberate. **Professional tax advice is strongly recommended in situations like this.**

## Examples

### An apartment across the river

You are domiciled in the District of Columbia and keep an Arlington apartment, spending most nights there. The place
of abode is maintained and your count passes 183 days, so you are an actual resident of Virginia.

### Driving through on the way south

You pass through Virginia on roughly 30 occasions each year heading to the Carolinas, never stopping for anything but
fuel. Those part-days are solely in transit to a destination outside the state, so they are excluded.

### A move that never completed

You leave Virginia for a long overseas contract but establish no new permanent home and intend to come back. Domicile
never changed, so you remain a domiciliary resident whatever your day count.

## FAQ

### What is the difference between an actual and a domiciliary resident?

A domiciliary resident has Virginia as their permanent home. An actual resident maintained a place of abode in
Virginia for more than 183 days of the tax year, whatever their domicile. Both are taxed as residents.

### Do days spent driving through Virginia count?

No, where the part-day was spent solely in transit to a destination outside the state. A day that includes anything
beyond passing through does count.

### Is the threshold 183 or 184 days?

The rule is an aggregate of more than 183 days, so day 184 is the first that qualifies.

### Can I be a Virginia resident and a resident of another state?

Yes. Actual residency in Virginia does not displace domicile elsewhere, so two states can tax the same year as a
resident year, with a credit reducing the overlap.

### How do I change my Virginia domicile?

You must abandon the Virginia domicile with no intention of returning and acquire a new one by being present in
another place intending to remain permanently or indefinitely. Both halves are needed.

### Does Virginia tax nonresidents?

Yes, on Virginia-source income. Residents are taxed on income from all sources wherever earned.
