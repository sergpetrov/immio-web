# US B-1/B-2 Visitor Visa

## Overview

:::callout
The single most misread thing about this visa is that its validity has nothing to do with how long you can stay. A
B-1/B-2 can be valid for **10 years**, but each time you arrive an officer decides how long you may remain — commonly
up to **6 months** — and records it on your **I-94**. That admission date is your actual deadline. Overstaying it by
enough triggers automatic multi-year bars on returning.
:::

| Key facts               |                                              |
|:------------------------|---------------------------------------------:|
| Stay limit              |            Up to 6 months per entry |
| Period / Window         |                Per admission, set on the I-94 |
| Counting                |                            Any part of a day |
| Additional requirements | Nonimmigrant intent, no employment |

## Understanding the rule

Three separate things get confused with each other, and keeping them apart is most of what this rule requires:

- **Visa validity** — how long the visa lets you *travel to* a port of entry and ask to be admitted. Often up to 10
  years, depending on your nationality's reciprocity arrangement.
- **The admission period** — how long you may actually *remain*, decided by a Customs and Border Protection officer at
  the border and recorded on your I-94. Usually up to 6 months for B-2, often shorter for B-1 depending on the
  business purpose.
- **Number of entries** — most B-1/B-2 visas are multiple-entry, so the visa can be reused until it expires.

A valid visa in your passport therefore guarantees nothing about how long you can stay on any given trip, and it isn't
a guarantee of entry either — admission is always the officer's decision.

The route also requires **nonimmigrant intent**: you have to satisfy the officer that you intend to leave and that
your ties abroad are strong enough to draw you back. B-1 covers business activity that isn't employment — meetings,
conferences, negotiations, contract work with a foreign employer. B-2 covers tourism, visiting family, and medical
treatment. Working for a US employer is not permitted on either.

## How to keep track

1. Your deadline is the **admit-until date on your I-94**, not the expiry date printed on the visa. Check the I-94
   record online after every entry, because officers sometimes grant less than the full period.
2. **Any part of a day** in the US counts toward your presence.
3. Departing and re-entering does not reset anything automatically. A short trip to Canada, Mexico or the Caribbean
   often results in being readmitted only for the balance of your original period rather than a fresh one.
4. Time spent in the US accumulates in the officer's view across trips, so repeat long visits are assessed as a
   pattern rather than in isolation.

Keep your I-94 records, boarding passes and entry stamps for every trip, and keep evidence of your ties abroad —
employment, property, family — since that's what supports nonimmigrant intent on each arrival.

## Edge cases

- **A ten-year visa is not ten years of stay.** This is the error that produces most accidental overstays. The visa is
  a travel document; the I-94 is the permission to remain.
- **Overstaying voids the visa automatically.** A visa held by someone who stays beyond their admission period becomes
  void, and a future application generally has to be made in your home country rather than anywhere convenient.
- **Extensions have to be filed before the I-94 expires.** An application for up to 6 further months is possible, but
  filing late is generally fatal to it, and leaving while it's pending abandons it.
- **Border-hopping to reset the clock is well recognised.** Frequent short exits followed by immediate re-entry is a
  known pattern, and it tends to attract shorter admissions or a refusal rather than a fresh six months.

## If you get this rule wrong

Unlawful presence accrues from the day after your I-94 expires, and the bars are automatic rather than discretionary: more than 180 days triggers a 3-year bar on returning, and a year or more triggers a 10-year bar, both applying once you leave. Your visa is also void from the moment of the overstay, so the next application has to be made from your country of nationality. **Professional immigration advice is strongly recommended in situations like this.**

## Examples

### A visa that outlives the stay

You hold a 10-year B-1/B-2 and are admitted in March with an I-94 running to September. Your visa remains valid for
years, but September is your deadline — staying into October starts unlawful presence even though the visa is nowhere
near expiring.

### A short hop that doesn't reset the count

You're admitted for 6 months, spend five of them in the US, then take a four-day trip to Mexico expecting a new six
months on return. You're readmitted for the remainder of your original period instead, leaving you weeks rather than
months.

### An extension filed too late

You decide in month six to stay longer and file for an extension a week after your I-94 expires. Because the
application wasn't filed while you still held status, it's very likely to be denied, and unlawful presence has already
begun accruing.

## FAQ

### If my visa is valid for 10 years, can I stay for 10 years?

No. The visa lets you travel to a US port of entry and request admission during those 10 years. How long you can
actually remain is set separately at each arrival and recorded on your I-94, commonly up to 6 months.

### Where do I find how long I'm allowed to stay?

On your I-94 admission record, which Customs and Border Protection publishes online. Check it after every entry — the
admit-until date there is what governs, and it isn't always the full period you expected.

### Can I leave and come back to get another 6 months?

Not reliably. A brief trip to a neighbouring country often results in readmission only for the balance of your original
admission, and a pattern of doing this tends to produce shorter admissions or refusal.

### Can I work on a B-1/B-2?

No. B-1 allows business activities that aren't employment — meetings, conferences, negotiations — and B-2 covers
tourism, family visits and medical treatment. Working for a US employer isn't permitted on either.

### Can I extend my stay?

You can apply to extend by up to 6 months, but the application has to be filed before your current I-94 expires.
Filing late generally sinks it, and departing while it's pending abandons it.

### Does having a visa mean I'll definitely be let in?

No. A visa permits you to travel to a port of entry and apply for admission. The officer there makes the decision, and
can admit you for a shorter period than you expected or refuse entry.
